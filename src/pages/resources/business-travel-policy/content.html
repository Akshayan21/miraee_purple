How to write a business travel policy your people will follow

A business travel policy is the set of rules that decides how employees book, what they can spend and who approves exceptions. This guide shows how to write one for a company of 300 to 1,500 people that travelers will actually follow, with a section-by-section checklist you can copy.

What makes a travel policy work?

A travel policy works when following it is the easiest way to book. Keep it short, put a number on every limit, name one place to book, and send only the exceptions to an approver. Then make the rules visible where people choose flights and hotels, so nobody has to open a document to stay inside them.

Why policies get ignored

Most companies have a travel policy. Fewer have one that shapes what people book. The usual reasons are practical, and they are fixable:

  • The policy lives in a document. Travelers read it once at onboarding, if at all. When they book three months later, they guess.
  • The limits are vague. "Reasonable" hotel rates mean something different to every traveler and every approver.
  • Every trip needs approval. When approvers see every booking, they stop reading them, and the exceptions get approved with the rest.
  • The booking tool is slower than a consumer site. Only about a third of business travelers prefer their company's booking tool to consumer sites.1 When the company channel is harder, people book elsewhere and expense it.

The last point is the one finance feels. 72% of travel buyers say travelers book cheaper hotels outside the company program.2 Those bookings are hard to see, hard to code and hard to help with when plans change. A good policy brings them back by making the company channel the simplest one, and by being clear about what happens to bookings made elsewhere.

Three decisions to make before you write

1. Who the policy covers. Employees, contractors, candidates and guests often travel on the company's budget. Decide whether one policy covers everyone or whether guests follow a shorter version.

2. Who owns it. In a company of 300 to 1,500 people, travel is often run by an operations, office or people lead on top of their day job, with finance setting the budget. Name one owner for the policy and one approver of changes to it. Write both names at the top.

3. Where booking happens. Name one booking channel for flights and hotels. If your agency handles complex trips, say which trips go to the agency and which are booked directly. A policy with two equal channels ends up with four.

How to write rules people can follow

Put a number on every limit. "Up to $250 a night in standard cities" can be checked by a traveler, an approver and a controller. "A reasonable rate" cannot.

Say what people can do first. "Book economy for flights under 6 hours" is easier to follow than a list of what is banned. It also reads as trust, which helps adoption.

Keep it to a few pages. Travelers need the booking channel, the limits and the approval rule. Put the detail finance needs, such as GL codes and cost centers, in the finance section or in the booking tool itself.

Write for the person booking at 10 p.m. Short sentences, one rule per line, and examples for the cases people ask about most: a conference hotel above the limit, a flight change, a trip that mixes business and personal days.

Say what happens to exceptions. An exception with a clear route, such as "ask your budget owner before you book", is followed far more often than a rule with no way around it.

Approvals: before every trip, or only for exceptions?

There are two common models, and most companies of this size move from the first to the second as they grow.

Pre-trip approval for every trip. Every booking waits for a manager. It gives control, and it creates a queue. Approvers see so many routine trips that the important ones blend in.

Approval only when something is out of policy. Trips inside the rules are booked straight away. Anything outside them, such as a hotel above the limit or a business-class fare, goes to an approver with the reason attached. Approvers see fewer requests, and each one needs a real decision.

If you choose the second model, the rules have to be applied at the moment of booking. That is where a booking tool that marks each option in or out of policy does the work a document cannot.

How to roll out a new travel policy

  1. Agree the numbers with finance. Flight, hotel and meal limits, the approval threshold and the GL codes for travel.
  2. Brief approvers first. They need to know what will reach them and what will not.
  3. Announce it in one message. The booking channel, the three limits people ask about most, and who to ask.
  4. Set it up in the booking tool before the announcement. The first booking after launch should already show the new rules.
  5. Answer questions for two weeks. Collect them; they tell you which rules are unclear.
  6. Review after the first month-end. Look at bookings outside the channel, exceptions approved and receipts missing. Adjust the rules that caused the most questions.

How to keep the policy current

Review the policy twice a year, and after any big change in how the company travels: a new office, a new client region, a new entity. Hotel limits drift fastest, because city rates move every season. Keep a short change log at the end of the policy so travelers and approvers can see what changed and when.

Questions

How long should a business travel policy be?

A few pages is enough for most companies of 300 to 1,500 people. Travelers need the booking channel, the limits and the approval rule. Put detail for finance, such as GL codes and cost centers, in a separate section or in the booking tool.

What is the difference between a business travel policy and a travel and expense policy?

A business travel policy covers how trips are booked and what they can cost. A travel and expense policy adds how expenses are submitted, checked and reimbursed. Many companies combine them. Our guide to the travel and expense policy covers the expense side.

Should every trip need approval?

For most growing companies, approval works best only when something is out of policy. Routine trips inside the rules are booked straight away, and approvers see the exceptions that need a decision.

How often should we update our travel policy?

Twice a year, and after any big change in how the company travels. Hotel limits need the most frequent attention because city rates change.

How do we get people to follow the policy?

Make the company booking channel the easiest way to book, put a number on every limit, and show the rules on the options people choose from. Our guide to travel policy compliance covers this in detail.
